Why documents

I have swapped the model underneath my Sunday system twice. Each time the system got better at the same job, because the job was written down in five files and the model was only the thing reading them.

Swap the files and you have a different system. Swap the model and you have the same system, slightly sharper.

So this is the part worth your afternoon.

The five

Fill them in this order. The first two take about twenty minutes between them. The other three are mostly choosing.

  1. household.md

    Who is in the house, the usual week, how "away" is marked.

    Turns a calendar readout into a briefing that knows nobody has Wednesday's pickup.

  2. sources.md

    Exactly what may be read, and who receives the result.

    The trust document. If it is not named here, it is not read.

  3. rules.md

    The judgment. What always goes in, what never does.

    The file that grows. Every line is a mistake that cannot happen twice.

  4. voice.md

    The register, the greeting, the sign-off, where a joke may go.

    An unread message is worse than no message.

  5. digest-template.md

    The shape. Day by day, then what to remember, then one line of radar.

    The message is what the family reads. Everything else is archive.

You do not need to finish them to start. A half-filled rules.md with three lines in it works on the first Sunday, and that is how it is supposed to go, because the rest of the lines get written by being wrong.

Getting the five written

You do not have to fill in templates. Paste one prompt into an ordinary Claude chat and it will interview you about your household and write all five for you.

Do this in a normal chat, not in the project. Only the finished documents go in the project, because instructions with blanks still in them make the system invent things to fill the gaps.

The setup prompt 
I want a weekly message that tells my household what is coming up: who is out,
who does drop-off and pickup, what is on, and anything that needs money or a
form. I will run it myself in this app every Sunday. There is no code and
nothing to install.

Before you write anything, interview me. Ask a few questions at a time, never
thirty. Assume no technical background and explain any word you would not say
out loud to a friend.

I need five documents at the end:

1. household.md         Who lives here, what each person is called in the
                        message, the usual week, who normally does drop-off and
                        pickup, what is standing, and what flexes.
2. sources.md           Which calendars may be read, which mail label or senders
                        may be read, and who receives the finished message.
                        Nothing outside this file gets read.
3. rules.md             What always goes in and what never does. Start me off
                        with sensible defaults and ask which ones I want changed.
4. voice.md             Who is speaking. Help me pick a character with a real
                        register, and make sure it does not sound like me. If my
                        family gets a message that reads as though I wrote it,
                        that is the wrong outcome.
5. digest-template.md   The shape: greeting, week label, day by day, a short
                        reminders tail, sign-off.

How I want you to work with me:

- One document at a time. Interview me for it, write it, show it to me, and
  change whatever I do not like before moving on to the next one.
- If I give you a vague answer, ask for the specific version. "Tuesdays are
  messy" is not something a system can follow.
- Facts come from me. Do not invent a name, a time, or a school event to make a

- Never ask me to paste a password or an account key into this chat. Nothing

At the end, give me all five documents in one block I can copy, in the order

Start by asking me about my household.

=== rules.md ===

## Always
- Money, forms and bookings go at the top, under REMEMBER, with the amount and
  deadline quoted exactly as the source wrote them.
- Check for a receipt before asking for money. If a confirmation arrived within
  a few days of the request, show it as done instead of asking again.
- If a key fact is missing, say so. "No date published, check the form" beats
  both omitting it and guessing.
- Dress-up days and bring-something days get flagged the week before, and again
  in the week. Never the night before.
- Every weekday line answers the same four questions: who is out, who does
  drop-off, who does pickup, what is on afterwards.
- If nobody covers a handoff, say so plainly. Do not resolve it, do not assume.

## Never
- Never invent a time, an amount or an address.
- Never repeat every email. Only items with a date, an amount, a deadline or an
  instruction survive.
- Never let an email's day-of-the-week mean this week. "Monday" means the Monday
  after it was sent.
- Never bury a fact inside a joke.
- Never cut a time, a place or an owner to make the message shorter. Cut the
  commentary instead.

## Radar
- One line. The single most important thing in the next two weeks.
- Worth it: camps, holidays, term boundaries, performances, anything needing
  money or a costume.

The instinct is to retype the line and move on. Do that and you will retype it again next week, and the week after, until you quietly stop using the thing.

After a month

The message stops being wrong in new ways and starts being wrong in the same two or three ways, which are the ways your household is genuinely ambiguous.

That is the point at which the automated version is worth a day of your time, and not before. Five filled-in documents and a Sunday habit beat a half-built system that nobody trusts.

Do it last. The page is the same gather wearing a better outfit. If the message is wrong, the page is wrong in colour and takes longer to read. ## What the page is for

The text is what the family reads walking out the door. The email is the
archive. The page is the one you sit down with once, with a coffee, and it has
a different job: **a clash should be visible before you have read a sentence.**

Everything below serves that.

---

## Inherited, without exception

Everything in `rules.md` and `voice.md` applies here. The format changed; the
judgment did not. In particular:

- Never invent a time, date, amount or address. Quote it, or say it is not
  published.
- Check for a receipt before asking for money again.
- A cancelled entry means it is off. Never restore it from the usual routine.
- An empty mail sweep means there was no mail. It never means invent some.
- A date named in an email is relative to the day that email was sent.
- Facts stay plain. The personality goes between them, never on them.

## One thing it does differently

`digest-template.md` allows one line of radar, because a text message has no
room to be wrong. The page gets a full four-week horizon table instead. A page
costs nothing to scroll.

### Clashes and decisions

Everything else on the page is reference. This block is the reason to build it.

- Walk the school sources first, then hold each item against the family
  calendar. Two things in the same hour is a clash. Two sources disagreeing
  about a date is a clash.
- One row per item: a checkbox, an owner's name, the decision in bold, then
  the detail that settles it.
- Name an owner always. "Everyone" is an owner. "Someone" is not.
- Order by what it costs to get wrong, not by date.
- Anything that changes the school day — a closure, a room move, medication,
  buses — goes at the top regardless of date. It affects the morning.

### Already handled

Bookings confirmed, payments receipted, appointments locked. One line each,
ticked. This is what stops the page reading as a list of demands.

**If nothing qualifies, cut the block.** Never pad it. If the mail sweep did
not run, say that instead — an unchecked list is not a clear one.

### The week and the weekend

- Monday to Friday as five columns. Each gets a strip naming who is away from
  home base, then a line answering **who does the morning and who does
  pick-up**, then the day's events.
- Name people. "Paul in Sydney", never "away". Never a bare "Home."
- The weekend names both parents too, day by day. Naming only the one who is
  travelling leaves the other unaccounted for.
- Every event keeps its time. An event without its time is an error, not a
  summary.

### The desk

One block per child: their name, year and school, then what the week actually
requires of them. Unread mail is worth naming — "Friday's newsletter is
unread" is a useful sentence.

Holidays, staff days and public holidays inside the next two weeks are phrased
as care impact: kids home, who is on.

## The Verify tag

The page's one invention, and the thing that stops it guessing.

A **Verify** tag means: two sources disagree, or a fact you need is not in any
source. It goes next to the item, and the item states both readings and what
each would cost.

> Cast B is listed Tuesday, Wednesday and Thursday. The family calendar only
> has Wednesday. If he is on Tuesday it sits on top of piano at 5:00pm; if
> Thursday, football starts at 4:00pm and arrival is 5:30pm. **Verify**

Two rules keep it meaningful:

- **Never tag something the sources settle.** A Verify tag on a known fact
  teaches you to ignore the tag.
- **Roughly seven a page, maximum.** Past that it stops meaning "look here".

## How it should look

Plain rules, not decoration. You can change all of this; it only has to hold
together.

- **One self-contained HTML file.** No scripts, no images, nothing to install.
- **Three typefaces by job:** a tight display face for headings, a plain sans
  for facts, a handwriting face for margin notes and the sign-off only.
- **Colour defined once at the top** and reused, so it survives dark mode.
  Any block with a tint must set its own text colour, or dark mode flips the
  text and leaves it sitting on the tint.
- **Heavy rules under the masthead and above the footer.** It should read like
  a newspaper, because a newspaper is a solved problem.
- **It must work on a phone.** Five columns become one. No sideways scrolling.
- **It must print.** Some people put it on the fridge.
- Two or three handwritten notes on the whole page, each an observation, never
  a label.

## Where a correction goes

Same rule as the other five. A page that gets something wrong sends you here,
or to `rules.md` if the message would have got it wrong too.

Never to the prompt. The page is this week's; the files are every week's.

Then, when you want one

run the Weekly Rundown

run the Sunday still gives you the text and the email, unchanged. The system is still the Sunday Rundown. Only the page has the new name.

The red Verify tags

The most useful thing on the page, and worth understanding before your first one. A Verify tag means the system found something it refuses to guess at, either two sources disagreeing or a fact you need that nobody published, and it states both readings and what each would cost.

Cast B is listed Tuesday, Wednesday and Thursday. The family calendar only has Wednesday. If he is on Tuesday it sits on top of piano at 5:00pm; if Thursday, football starts at 4:00pm and arrival is 5:30pm. Verify

That is not the system failing. That is the system finding the thing you would otherwise have discovered on Tuesday at 5:25pm.

Six or seven of them means it is working. Twenty means household.md is vague. None, on a busy week, means it guessed, and that is worth checking rather than enjoying.

When the page is wrong

Same rule, one more file. A block in the wrong order, missing, or too long goes in rundown-template.md. A bad call about what mattered still goes in rules.md. Never fix the page.

It is a page about your children. Their names, their schools, where they are on which afternoon, and which nights the house has one adult in it. If you publish it to get a link, keep that link to yourself and share it deliberately.

Two things to decide now

Both are painful to change later.

Who is on the receiving end. Anyone who gets the message needs to be able to act on it. People who receive it out of politeness stop reading by week three, and then you have a system that is technically working and practically ignored.

Whether it sounds like you. It should not. A weekly message that convincingly impersonates you to your own family is a strange thing to have built, and you will only notice once somebody replies to it.
